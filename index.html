Abstract

        Bats are under strict protection laws in the UK and EU as they are important pollinators and pest control. In the UK all 18 species
        are at risk of extinction, so conservationists try to locate bat roosts to monitor bat activity and population. The use of mathematics
        in locating roosts helps reduce labour costs and search time and lessens the chances of accidentally disturbing the population they 
        are trying to study. Using bat detectors, Cardiff School of Mathematics and the University of Sussex have created an algorithm that 
        allows them to make likelihood maps showing predicted locations of horseshoe bat roosts in an area, reducing the search area. 
        Henley et al (2016)’s predicted locations were very accurate for 5/6 of the studies by Finch et al (2016). This new method could help 
        conservationists across the globe to better locate and protect bat colonies from extinction.

Introduction

        Bats play a vital role in our ecosystems, from dispersing seeds to pest control and pollination, but are currently facing extinction 
        due to chemical, light and air pollution, climate change, onshore wind turbines, building works and roost disturbances alongside other 
        factors (bat conservation trust 2021). Locating bat roosts has been challenging conservationists for years, and sometimes in their 
        search they may accidentally disturb the maternity roosts they set out to study, which can result in decreased chances of adult female 
        bat survival and reduced success of breeding. Locating roosts in a safer and less time-consuming, more cost-efficient manner could 
        help reduce chances of accidental roost disturbances and allow conservationists to better record bat activity and population. In the 
        research presented by Henley et al (2024), audio detectors, a bat movement model and some algorithms were used to create roost 
        likelihood maps. This helps to reduce labour costs, search time and search area, and point out likely locations so that 
        conservationists could take extra care in those areas, lowering chances of accidental disturbances. This article will firstly discuss 
        research and methods used by Henley et al (2024) in more depth, followed by an assessment of the outcomes the model’s future utility.

Analysis and discussion

        Henley et al (2024) used data from the study by Finch et al (2016) to create their models, and since bats use echolocation Finch et al 
        (2016) decided to use passive acoustic bat detectors - which are generally used to identify bat calls and species - to detect bats in 
        an area. “Surveys are conducted by placing detectors …  detectors then record [the bats] … The coordinates of each detector are noted 
        and a series of recordings is produced, providing a spatio-temporal map of bat presence. Furthermore, the species … can be identified …”
        - Henley et al (2024) This means that using recordings of bats picked up by the detectors and the detector locations (measured in 
        eastings and northings), a map can be made illustrating areas of higher and lower levels of activity. This may be helpful in locating 
        roosts since each bat returns to rest, coming and going frequently meaning the roost should be in a very active area. Since each species
        uses different frequencies and calls, this can help identify which species the colony is. Finch et al (2016)’s study looked at 6 roost 
        locations of greater horseshoe bats in the UK, and Henley et al (2024) noted that the timeframe for recordings could be limited to the 
        first 90 minutes after sunset as this is the most active time for this species. 

        Using a deterministic partial differential equation (PDE) diffusion model Henley et al (2024) were able to estimate an approximation 
        for the proportion of bat calls at each detector using a predicted roost location. Since bats will spread out to forage after sunset, 
        they can be modelled as diffusive particles. Bats don’t tend to stray too far from their roost so it is assumed that the detectors with 
        the least activity will be furthest from the roost, and using the detector information they created likelihood maps for each location, 
        shown in figure 1 below.
